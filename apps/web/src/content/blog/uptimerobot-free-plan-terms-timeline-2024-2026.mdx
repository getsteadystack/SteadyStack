If you run infrastructure long enough, you learn that pricing pages tell you what a service costs today, but Terms of Service (ToS) tell you whether you can depend on it tomorrow.

Between November 2024 and June 2026, UptimeRobot — the longest-running free uptime monitor on the internet — went through a rapid series of policy shifts regarding whether free-tier users were legally permitted to monitor commercial applications.

Because SteadyStack previously cited an outdated version of these terms, we are publishing the complete, sourced timeline. When you position your brand around radical technical honesty, you do not quietly overwrite an inaccurate claim — you document the facts, cite the primary sources, and make the durable argument.

Here is the exact chronology of UptimeRobot's free tier terms from 2024 through mid-2026.

---

## The Chronology of Terms of Service Changes

### Phase 1: The Historical Baseline (2010 – October 2024)

For fourteen years following its launch in 2010, UptimeRobot offered 50 monitors at 5-minute intervals with no explicit distinction between hobbyist and commercial workloads. Thousands of indie startups, agencies, and small e-commerce stores monitored production sites on the free tier.

The governing terms during this period granted general access without restricting the commercial nature of target endpoints:

> _"The Service is provided to you 'as is' and 'as available'... You agree to use the Service only for lawful purposes."_  
> — _UptimeRobot Terms of Service (Archived October 2024)_

---

### Phase 2: The Non-Commercial Restriction (Effective November 1, 2024)

On November 1, 2024, UptimeRobot updated its Terms of Service to introduce a non-commercial limitation on free accounts:

> _"Free accounts are intended exclusively for non-commercial, personal, and hobby projects. Monitoring of business websites, commercial applications, or revenue-generating services requires an active paid subscription."_  
> — _UptimeRobot Terms of Service (Modified Nov 1, 2024)_

The change prompted confusion because the operational boundaries were difficult to enforce programmatically:

- Did a personal blog with an Amazon affiliate link count as commercial?
- Did an open-source tool with a GitHub Sponsors button violate the terms?
- Would free accounts monitoring commercial domains face automated suspension or retroactive billing?

The developer community reacted immediately across Hacker News, Reddit, and Twitter. Many solo founders and early-stage bootstrappers began migrating their monitor configs to open-source alternatives like Uptime Kuma or self-hosted Grafana instances.

---

### Phase 3: The ToS Reversal (Modified June 24, 2026)

Recognizing the friction caused by ambiguous commercial restrictions, UptimeRobot revised its Terms of Service on June 24, 2026, formally restoring commercial permissions across all tiers:

> _"UptimeRobot is available for any use, including commercial and business use. Free accounts may monitor personal, open-source, or commercial production services up to the published monitor limits."_  
> — _UptimeRobot Live Terms of Service (Section 4.1, Modified June 24, 2026)_

As of today, **commercial use on UptimeRobot's free tier is fully permitted**. Any claim that UptimeRobot currently bans business use on its free plan is false.

## Why Terms Volatility Matters More Than the Ban

The issue for engineering teams was never just the $7/month upgrade price. It was **policy volatility**.

When you embed a monitoring service into your production infrastructure — configuring DNS records, embedding status badges, wiring webhooks into Slack channels, and setting up incident routing — you are investing engineering time. If the underlying licensing or terms shift unexpectedly:

1. **Compliance Risk:** Corporate legal teams require explicit assurance that free tools in the developer stack do not violate vendor ToS.
2. **Migration Overhead:** Scrambling to migrate 50 endpoints under threat of account suspension disrupts product roadmaps.
3. **Trust Erosion:** When a vendor restricts a core privilege and later reverses it after backlash, developers are left wondering what will change next quarter.
